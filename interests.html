Research Interests

We study how evolutionary processes shape biological diversity. Our research program combines evolutionary genetics, genomics, comparative methods, and field studies to study traits that pit short-term vs. long-term evolutionary success. Specifically, we are interested in traits such as plant reproductive systems and ecological generalization/specialization and their effects on speciation and extinction rates.

Breeding and Mating System Evolution in Flowering Plants

Flowering plants exhibit remarkable diversity in sexual and mating systems, ranging from obligate outcrossing to predominant self-fertilization and from hermaphroditism to multiple forms of sexual polymorphism. We study the genetic basis, evolutionary origins, and long-term consequences of this variation.

A major focus is self-incompatibility (SI), the genetic system that enables plants to recognize and reject their own pollen. SI loci are among the most polymorphic regions of plant genomes because rare variants are strongly favored by selection. Their exceptional allelic diversity and deep evolutionary histories make them powerful systems for studying population history, molecular co-evolution, transitions between outcrossing and self-fertilization, as well as their effects on lineage diversification.

We use genomic and evolutionary approaches to identify genes underlying reproductive variation and to reconstruct how mating systems change through time. We are particularly interested in whether the transition from outcrossing to self-fertilization represents an evolutionary “dead end,” as proposed by Stebbins, or whether its consequences depend on ecological and evolutionary context.

We also investigate the genetic architecture and adaptive significance of mating-system differences among closely related species. Using transcriptomic/genomic approaches, genomic mapping, comparative analyses, and field experiments, we ask whether repeated evolutionary transitions arise through parallel changes in the same genes or through different genetic routes to similar phenotypes.

Macroevolutionary Inference

A second portion of the research program concerns the methods of inference of evolutionary fate of lineages, with long-term/short-term trade-offs ecological specialization as a frequent study subject. It is commonly thought that specialists may gain advantages from adaptation to particular environments or resources, but specialization may also restrict geographic distributions, reduce ecological flexibility, and increase extinction risk.

Using comparative data from varied organisms, such as flowering plants and passerine birds, we test how ecological specialization and generalization influence rates of speciation, extinction, and trait evolution. This work combines phylogenetic comparative methods with improved approaches for reconstructing character evolution, ancestral states, and detecting variation in diversification rates.

Across research areas, we are interested in developing and applying methods that connect molecular evolution, organismal traits, ecology, and diversification. Current collaborative work also examines the co-evolution of interacting genes and proteins and the detection of positively selected molecular interactions.
